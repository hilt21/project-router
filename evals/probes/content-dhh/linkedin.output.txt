An old mental model can survive years of new information.

In “Seven years from China,” David Heinemeier Hansson describes returning after seven years away. On his trip, electric vehicles, quieter streets and a highly automated Xiaomi factory made changes he had followed remotely feel concrete.

He also acknowledges that a one-week visit offers a selective view.

The lesson I take for engineering and product leaders is about keeping our assumptions current.

When change arrives in small increments, each update can feel unsurprising. The cumulative shift is easy to miss. Familiarity can leave us comparing today's market with a version we remember.

A quarterly deck may report each change accurately while leaving that mental model intact.

Periodically, return to the work itself:

• Use a competing product for a complete workflow.
• Sit beside a customer through a difficult task.
• Visit the team or production line behind a performance claim.
• Write down what now feels ordinary that would have surprised you three years ago.

Bring those observations back to your data. Check them against other perspectives. Then revisit the assumptions behind your roadmap, architecture and investment decisions.

A useful outcome of a visit is a precise revision: which assumption changed, what evidence changed it, and what decision should follow?

Familiarity needs maintenance. Schedule time to see the current version of the world you think you know.
